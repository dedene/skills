## EB: Element Brainstorm

The most useful mode in practice. It is cheap, fast, and gets the creator unstuck without rendering anything. Keep every line very short. Prefer brevity over variety.

### Step 1: Text elements (10–15, one short sentence each)

Probe the idea through four lenses and label each line with its lens:

- **Inner voice**: what people feel, think, or say about this. Look for hidden truths and relatable moments ("What if it fails and everyone knows it was me?").
- **Data**: results, timelines, odds, statistics, costs. Only use numbers with a source you can verify now; otherwise phrase it as a pattern ("most people quit in the first week") and never invent a statistic.
- **Comparisons**: now/later, expectation/reality, before/after, success/failure, effort/outcome, problem/solution, risk/reward, past/present, talk/action.
- **Takeaway**: advice or a warning. Do this, avoid that, try this, watch out for that.

### Step 2: Visual elements (~12, one tight line each)

Sketch each visual in words and say where the text from step 1 would sit. Spread across five families:

- **Context**: the real-world scene where the idea lives. Cluttered inbox, chaotic calendar, 40 browser tabs, a to-do list, a chat thread, a messy desk.
- **Geometric abstraction**: shapes that carry meaning. Many small squares vs one big one, a tangle vs a straight line, stacked blocks, loops, funnels, grids, circles crossing an axis.
- **Classic elements**: familiar chart grammar. Growth curve, milestones on a line, process A vs process B, bar chart, pie chart, Venn overlap, flow of boxes and arrows, XY graph.
- **Characters**: personify a force. A doubt goblin on someone's shoulder, an inbox that grows arms, a stick figure with a thought bubble.
- **Metaphors**: a physical thing that behaves like the idea. A leaking bucket, a snowball rolling downhill, a half-built bridge, a seed vs a tree, a compass vs a map.

Push toward unexpected directions, then filter hard for clarity and impact.

### EB output shape

```
Core insight: <one sentence>

Text elements
1. Feeling: …
2. Thought: "…"
…

Visual elements
1. Context: … — text "…" on …
2. Geometric: …
…
```

## VB: Visual Brainstorm

1. **Core insight first.** State the main takeaway in one sentence.
2. Run EB first. In standalone VB, run it silently. In the default workflow, the compact EB is already printed.
3. Print the chosen **style block once** (from `style-guide.md`) above the concepts, labeled "Style for all concepts". Then write **10 concepts, one per lane**, numbered by lane:
   1. Visualize context (a real scene)
   2. Geometric abstraction: shapes/quantity/contrast
   3. Geometric abstraction: flow, loop, or funnel
   4. Timeline
   5. Milestones or path (checkpoints along a road, a trail of footprints)
   6. Process comparison or XY graph
   7. Venn / overlap
   8. Metaphor comparison (two states of one object: intact vs broken, closed vs open)
   9. Single metaphor (one object carries the whole idea)
   10. 2×2 matrix
   A lane is weak when forcing the idea into it would hurt clarity (e.g. a Venn for a sequence, or a timeline for a static trade-off). Replace a weak lane with a character or a second metaphor and say which lane was swapped.
4. **Format per concept:** one short paragraph, no concept name. It describes the layout (what sits left/right/top/bottom), the objects, and every on-image string in double quotes. Don't repeat the style block inside each paragraph; at Render time the full block is pasted in front of the chosen concept. Use as few words and as few separate text placements as possible.
5. After the 10, add **Top 3** (by lane number): one line each on why it passes one-idea, clarity, novelty, and resonance.

Example concept paragraph:
> Two identical buckets side by side. Left: a bucket full of holes under a running tap, water spraying out, labeled "MORE LEADS". Right: the same bucket with the holes patched, filling up, labeled "FIX THE LEAKS FIRST". No other text.

## CB: Critique & Caption

Input: a visual, plus a title/caption if one exists. First understand the core idea from the whole image.

### Scorecard

Score 1–10 on each criterion. Give one short, concrete sentence per criterion about the single change that would raise the score most.

| Criterion | What a 10 looks like |
|---|---|
| One idea | Exactly one takeaway, no side plots |
| Novelty | Fresh angle or combination; feels new even if the idea isn't |
| Resonance | Audience recognizes their own feeling or situation |
| Clarity over cleverness | Understood in 3–4 seconds by a stranger |
| Amplification | Text adds a punch, question, or advice beyond the picture |
| Flow | Reads in order; words and shapes click together |

Rules for the critic:
- Stay inside the creator's concept. Don't propose a different visual.
- Simplicity and clarity are top values. Never suggest adding icons, decoration, more colors or more detail unless that fixes a clarity failure.
- Honest scores: a mediocre visual gets a 5. These are guides, not rules. The real test is showing it to a person.

### Captions

Write 6 caption options. A caption amplifies the idea: a sharper consequence, a question, a challenge or a twist. It must not restate what the image already says. Keep them short and punchy, mostly one line. If the user likes one, offer 3 variations in that direction (shorter, one sentence, different verb).

Worked example of amplifying: visual of a tangled line labeled "BUSY" next to a straight arrow labeled "PRODUCTIVE" → caption "Your calendar can't tell the difference. You can."

### Comparison wording

For two-sided visuals, generate pairs that mirror each other in rhythm and length and set up an "aha" (e.g. "Ideas are cheap / Shipping is rare" → "Everyone ideates / Few iterate", "Thinking feels like progress / Finishing is progress"). Push past the first obvious pair.

## Chapter expansion (dense source → concepts)

Use for book chapters, long articles, transcripts, or jargon-heavy text.

**Step 1: Expand.** Brief bullets on:
- emotions and common thoughts linked to it
- known facts, statistics, patterns (real ones only)
- comparisons and contrasts
- the lesson or takeaway
- perspectives: timeline-based, loop-based, comparison-based, pattern-based (e.g. Dream → Try → Fail → Learn → Try again)

**Step 2: Four concepts.** Write four different but related concept statements, each a short plain paragraph that:
- carries a single idea, insight or takeaway
- stands on its own without referring back to the source
- uses simple words (roughly 10-year-old reading level)
- aims for novelty and relatability

Then run EB or VB on whichever concept the user (or you, stating why) picks. Reading the four statements often triggers a new angle, such as spotting that the idea is really a loop. That's the point of this step.

## Idea sourcing (only if asked)

Where good raw ideas come from: a running topic list; personal breakthroughs, struggles and unspoken feelings; anything that caught attention while reading or scrolling (and the personal story it triggers); the most popular posts from creators in the same topics (pre-validated ideas); quote collections from books on the topic; and AI prompts about the audience's obstacles, misconceptions, surprising insights and actionable ideas. Keep capture scrappy: notes and snapshots of shapes and metaphors seen in daily life (a queue, a traffic light, a half-full glass).
